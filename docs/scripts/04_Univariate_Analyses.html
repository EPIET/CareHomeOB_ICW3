1. Factors associated with becoming a case

You will start the analysis by exploring associations between some non-food variables and becoming a case. Several methods are available to you, but let’s explore the one you already now first, by using gtsummary::tbl_summary():

Action — Explore the non-food variables age, sex and group as the first step

  • Build a tbl_summary() stratified by case variable and include the add_p() call to perform a statistical comparison

  • Is any of the variables associated with becoming a case in our data?

Action — Can you identify the statistical test employed for each variable? Why would the Wilcoxon rank sum test be used here?

CautionSee the solution 
# Perform statistical comparisson table using tbl_summary()
exp_agesexgroup_table <- clean %>% 
  select(case, age, sex, group) %>%   # variables of interest only
  mutate(case = recode_values(
  case,
  0 ~ "Non-case",
  1 ~ "Case")) %>% 
  tbl_summary(by = case) %>%          # stratifying variable
  add_p() %>%                         # perform statistical comparison
  bold_labels()                       # add some visuals

# see table
exp_agesexgroup_table

The Wilcoxon test was decided by tbl_summary() as it identified age to be non-normally distributed. You can also check it yourself with base r functions:

# Check if age overall follows a normal distribution:
shapiro.test(clean$age)

# Perform Wilcoxon rank sum test on age and sex:
wilcox.test(age ~ case, data = clean)

# Chi square test by hand, for demosntration
chisq.test(clean$sex, clean$case)
chisq.test(clean$group, clean$case)

Conclusion: age, sex and group were not significantly associated with becoming a case, i.e., we have not enough evidence to conclude that there was some differentiated exposure or behavior between attendees based on these characteristics. This can be interpreted like: they were all exposed to the same food items and showed no preference based on their age, sex or group

2. Risk associated with food exposure

Item by Item

To asses the relationship between the different food items and the risk of becoming a case, we will calculate risk ratios and 95% confidence intervals, food by food. For this, we will use the EpiStats package, one of the many existing libraries for epidemiology:

The CS() function builds the 2x2 table between an exposure and an outcome, that we declare as quoted arguments

# Calculate risk diffrence, risk ratios and attributable fraction among exposed
EpiStats::CS(clean,    # data
             "case",   # outcome variable, quoted
             "jamon")  # exposure variable, quoted

Action — Perform the univariate risk calculation over two other food items of your choice and interpret the results

ImportantInterpret the results of CS()

The outputs of CS()consist on two tables called $df1 and $df2 when printed in the console.

The first one is the 2x2 contingency table with exposed-unexposed and cases-non cases and the overall risk in each strata. For jamon, the risk of becoming a case was 30% for those who ate it, and 32% for those who didn’t.

The second table contain different risk estimators together with their 95% confidence interval:

  • Risk difference: is the crude difference between the risk of becoming a case in the exposed and the unexposed.

  • Risk ratio: relative measure of how higher or lower the risk among one group compared with the other.

  • Attributable fraction among the exposed and the population: proportion of the cases that can be explained with eating jamon.

We cannot establish the existence of an association between eating jamon and becoming a case.

All items at once (univariate)

You could also explore all food items at once with a single function to get a better overview in a single go, by creating a vector of food variables and passing it to the CSTable() function of the same EpiStats libraries:

# Food vector
food <- c("russian_salad", "spanish_tortilla", "jamon", "chicken",
          "watermelon", "wine", "water", "croquettes", "cheese")

# Risk ratio table for all food items
CSTable(clean, 
        "case", 
        food)    # vector of exposures

3. Dose-response relationship

The questionnaire also included questions about the amount of each food item consumed, which gives us the chance of exploring whether a dose-response relationship exist. We will only explore russian_salad and spanish_tortilla as the only food items with increased risk.

You will use the library epitools with the function riskratio() because CS() can only handle binary (i.e., 2x2) tables, while dose-response include three categories already. The function works quite similarly:

# Calculate Risk Ratios by each consumption level, and associated p values
epitools::riskratio(clean$russian_saladD,                     
                    clean$case,                     
                    conf.level = 0.95)

epitools::riskratio(clean$spanish_tortillaD,                     
                    clean$case,                     
                    conf.level = 0.95)

Action — Can you affirm that a dose-response relationship exist for any of the two food items? How does it affect your overall hypotheses of association?
