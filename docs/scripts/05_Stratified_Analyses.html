1. Consider Confounding and Effect Modification

Once you have identified food items associated with an elevated risk of becoming a case, the next step is to whether those associations are distorted or modified by a third variable.

Confounding and effect modification are two distinct phenomena, both involving a third variable, but leading to very different interpretation:

  • Confounding is a distortion of the observed association. The third variable is associated with both both the exposure and the outcome, and the crude estimate does not reflect the true effect (bias). When we stratify by the confounder, the stratum-specific estimates are similar to each other but differ from the crude estimate. We adjust for the confounding variable to control it.

  • Effect modification (aka interaction) is a real phenomenon: the effect of the exposure on the outcome genuinely differs across levels of the third variable. When we stratify, the stratum-specific estimates differ meaningfully from each other. Effect modification is not corrected because it is not a bias, it is described and reported openly.

We investigate both through stratification. For medium-to-large datasets, exploring variable by variable becomes impractical and we turn to regression methods. Here we will use csinter() from EpiStats to manually explore how the association varies across the levels of a third one.

Spanish tortilla as exposure of interest

Spanish tortilla came out second in RR during univariable analysis, suggesting a potential association between eating tortilla and becoming a case. A plausible explanation is that people who ate russian salad also ate Spanish tortilla, so the effect of tortilla is a simple reflection of the effect of russian salad (i.e., confounding). Alternatively, the tortilla effect could genuinely differ depending on whether russian salad was eaten (i.e., effect modification).

Stratification lets us distinguish between the two. The question is: does the association between Spanish tortilla and becoming a case hold once we account for the russian salad consumption?

CautionSee the solution

Step 1 — Any effect modification? Compare the two stratum-specific RRs:

  • Russian salad = 1 → RR = 1.58 (95% CI 0.91–2.75)
  • Russian salad = 0 → RR = 1.04 (95% CI 0.28–3.83)

Step 2 — Any confounding? Compare the crude and the adjusted RR:

  • Crude RR = 2.14 (95% CI 1.28–3.57)
  • MH-adjusted RR = 1.46 (95% CI 0.88–2.43)
  • Relative change = –31.83 %

A relative change above ~10–20 % is a common rule of thumb for meaningful confounding, and here it is roughly 32 %. The adjusted CI also crosses 1. So the apparent association between Spanish tortilla and becoming a case is largely explained by Russian salad consumption: once we account for who ate salad, tortilla no longer shows a clear independent effect.

# Chi-square test of association between two binary variables
chisq.test(clean$spanish_tortilla, clean$russian_salad)

    Pearson's Chi-squared test with Yates' continuity correction

data:  clean$spanish_tortilla and clean$russian_salad
X-squared = 16.173, df = 1, p-value = 5.78e-05

The Chi-squared test clearly identifies a correlation between the two food items, with a p-value of 5.78e-05 (0.0000578)

Why does this matter, and what should we see? For a variable to act as a confounder, it must satisfy three conditions:

  1. Be associated with the exposure (here, Spanish tortilla).
  2. Be associated with the outcome (here, becoming a case), independently of the exposure.
  3. Not be on the causal pathway between exposure and outcome.

This check addresses the condition 1. We know condition 2 from the univariable analysis, and condition 3 is plausible. If people who ate Russian salad were also much more likely to eat Spanish tortilla, then part of the crude “tortilla effect” we saw earlier was really the Russian salad effect leaking through — which is exactly the pattern of confounding we observed when the adjusted RR dropped from 2.14 to 1.46.

Step 1 — Any effect modification? Compare the two stratum-specific RRs for Russian salad:

  • Crude RR = 4.39 (95% CI 2.28–8.44)
  • MH-adjusted RR = 4.05 (95% CI 1.97–8.32)
  • Relative change = –7.68 %

The adjusted estimate is almost identical to the crude one (change well below the ~10 % rule of thumb), and the CI remains far from 1. Spanish tortilla does not confound the Russian salad – case association.

Step 3 — Put the two stratified analyses side by side

Tortilla as exposure Russian salad as exposure
Crude RR 2.14 (1.28–3.57) 4.39 (2.28–8.44)

Adjusted CI crosses 1? Yes No

This asymmetry is the key diagnostic result of the stratified analysis:

  • The crude tortilla effect collapses when we account for Russian salad consumption → the association was largely explained by the fact that tortilla-eaters also tended to eat Russian salad.
  • The crude Russian salad effect persists almost unchanged when we account for tortilla consumption → Russian salad is associated with disease independently of tortilla.

Taken together, the evidence points to Russian salad as the likely vehicle of the outbreak, with Spanish tortilla acting as a “passenger” exposure whose apparent effect was confounded by salad consumption.
